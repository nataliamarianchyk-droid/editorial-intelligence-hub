The average repeat-purchase rate hides a clock. Customers acquired last week have had less time to buy again than customers acquired six months ago.

Comparing those groups directly can make a healthy new cohort appear weak or an old cohort appear stronger simply because it has had more opportunity to return. A retention programme built on that comparison may send unnecessary discounts or declare an acquisition channel unsuccessful before the buying cycle has played out.

Cohorts make the observation window visible. They ask not only whether people return, but when, at what economic cost and with what contribution.

## Choose a cohort definition that matches the question

For acquisition quality, group customers by their first purchase period. For product replenishment, group them by a relevant first product or purchase event. For campaign evaluation, preserve the acquisition context without assuming it proves the channel caused future behaviour.

State whether cancelled or fully refunded first orders count, how guest orders are linked and how subscription renewals are treated. Inconsistent customer identity can make one person look like several first-time buyers.

Shopify's customer reports include cohort analysis, and its documentation warns that some customer metrics use the customer's entire order history rather than only orders within the selected report period. Read the metric definition before interpreting a November report as November-only behaviour. [1](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/reports-and-analytics/shopify-reports/report-types/default-reports/customers-reports)

## Compare customers at the same age

A 30-day repeat rate should include only cohorts with a full 30 days of observation. A 90-day metric should not treat a ten-day-old customer as a non-repeater for the remaining 80 days.

The following table is a hypothetical example, not store data or an industry benchmark:

| First-purchase cohort | Customers | Repeated within 30 days | 30-day repeat rate | 90-day status |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Mature cohort A | 200 | 40 | 20% | Fully observable |
| Mature cohort B | 200 | 50 | 25% | Fully observable |
| Recent cohort C | 200 | Not yet complete | Not yet reportable | Incomplete |

Do not replace incomplete values with zero. Mark them unavailable or analyse time-to-event methods that account for right censoring. A simple age-aligned cohort table is often more useful than a sophisticated model whose assumptions nobody understands.

## Separate timing from eventual propensity

A consumable product and a durable item have different repeat patterns. A long gap does not necessarily mean the customer is lost. Look at the distribution of time between first and second purchases for comparable products and customer groups.

Median time among repeat purchasers alone has a limitation: it excludes people who never return. Report it alongside the proportion who repeat by a fixed horizon. Otherwise a small group of fast repeaters can make retention look strong while most customers remain inactive.

Inspect whether the second purchase is a genuine new demand event, a replacement, an exchange or a subscription renewal. Those events may have different commercial meanings.

## Measure contribution, not just return visits

Discounts can pull purchases forward or subsidise orders that would have happened anyway. A higher repeat rate does not automatically mean a stronger customer relationship or higher profit.

Track net revenue, variable costs and contribution after retention spend over a defined horizon. Keep returns and incentives in the same economic view. Shopify's profit-report guidance provides a reference for cost recording and adjustments, but additional fulfilment and marketing costs may be needed for your contribution definition. [2](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/reports-and-analytics/shopify-reports/report-types/default-reports/profit-reports)

## Test the intervention, not the audience label

Customers selected for a retention campaign may already be more likely to return. Comparing recipients with all other customers can credit the campaign for a difference created by selection.

Where appropriate, randomise a holdout among eligible customers. Compare repeat contribution and timing over a defined period, not only email opens or immediate coupon redemption. Keep the offer, eligibility rules and observation window documented before seeing results.

Respect contact preferences and avoid using sensitive inferences merely to sharpen targeting. A commercially useful cohort still needs an appropriate communication policy.

## Build the calendar from buying behaviour

Use the observed window to decide when education, replenishment reminders or service support might be useful. Do not convert one median into an identical send date for every product and customer.

For the acquisition economics, read [Shopify ROAS Is Not Your Contribution Margin](/ecommerce/shopify-contribution-margin). A good retention calendar follows the customer's plausible need, while a good cohort report checks whether acting on that need creates additional contribution.
