A campaign can report a strong return on ad spend while the orders it acquires leave little money to fund the business.

ROAS divides attributed revenue by advertising spend. Contribution asks a different question: what remains after the variable costs associated with earning that revenue? Confusing those measures encourages teams to scale orders that look attractive in an advertising dashboard but are economically weak.

The solution is not to discard ROAS. It is to stop asking it to answer a profit question it was not designed to answer.

## Agree the economic definition

Start with a written definition of net sales and contribution. Decide how discounts, refunds, taxes and shipping revenue are treated. Then identify the variable costs included in your contribution calculation: product cost, fulfilment, payment fees, shipping subsidy and other order-dependent costs.

Different businesses use different contribution layers. Make the layer explicit rather than labelling several incompatible calculations "profit". Fixed overhead, salaries and other excluded costs still matter to the business even when they are not included in this particular measure.

Shopify's profit-report documentation explains that cost per item must be recorded for relevant products and variants and discusses discounts and refunds. That is a useful starting point, not a complete contribution model for every store. ## Work through one order cohort

Consider the following hypothetical acquisition cohort. These are invented figures for explanation, not an NM Insight campaign or a Shopify benchmark.

| Item | Illustrative amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Net sales after discounts and refunds, excluding tax | €10,000 |
| Product cost | €4,000 |
| Fulfilment, fees and shipping subsidy | €2,000 |
| Contribution before advertising | €4,000 |
| Advertising spend | €3,000 |
| Contribution after advertising | €1,000 |

Using net sales as the revenue base, ROAS is approximately 3.33. Contribution before advertising is 40% of net sales. The simplified break-even ROAS is 1 divided by 0.40, or 2.5, before fixed costs and any omitted variable costs.

This threshold is not universal. It changes with product mix, discounts, returns and the cost definition. It also assumes the revenue and advertising spend refer to the same economic scope.

## Reconcile store records with attributed revenue

An ad platform may report revenue under its own attribution window and currency settings. Store records describe orders and adjustments. Those totals should not be assumed identical.

Compare like-for-like dates, currencies, refund timing and tax treatment. Watch for duplicated purchase events, order edits and gross revenue that has not yet been adjusted for returns. Separate the accounting ledger from attribution claims rather than choosing whichever number looks better.

Attributed revenue also does not establish incrementality. Some credited customers would have purchased without the campaign. A profitable attributed cohort can still overstate the campaign's causal contribution.

## Segment the margin drivers

Average margin can hide a product mix shift. A campaign that attracts buyers to a low-margin product may generate the same ROAS as a higher-margin campaign while contributing less.

Inspect product families, discount use, new versus returning customers and markets where delivery economics differ. Keep enough volume in each segment to avoid overinterpreting a handful of orders.

Record acquisition costs consistently. Decide whether creative production, agency fees and other costs belong in the operating view, and avoid comparing a fully loaded cost in one channel with media-only spend in another.

## Treat repeat purchase as evidence, not a rescue story

A weak first order may be acceptable when reliable repeat contribution follows. It is not acceptable simply because the team hopes lifetime value will improve.

Use mature acquisition cohorts, a defined payback horizon and actual repeat contribution. Avoid multiplying today's average order value by an assumed number of future purchases. Recent cohorts have not yet had time to prove those purchases will occur.

For a disciplined repeat-purchase view, read [Retention Cohorts: When Does a Second Purchase Actually Happen?](/ecommerce/retention-cohorts-repeat-purchase).

## Put guardrails beside the bidding target

Keep ROAS as an operational signal, but add contribution after advertising, return exposure and cash payback to the review. Agree when a campaign can scale and when margin deterioration requires a pause.

Read [DTC Attribution After ATT](/ecommerce/dtc-attribution-after-att) for the causal limits of attributed reporting. The commercial question is not whether the platform can claim revenue. It is whether the acquired demand leaves enough economic value to support the next decision.
